At $250,000 a year, the honest answer is: it depends less on your income than on the complexity of your finances and what you'd actually use an advisor for.

Where an advisor clearly earns their fee
If you have a genuinely complicated situation — a mix of taxable accounts, registered accounts (RRSP/TFSA), possibly a pension, real estate, and questions about retirement timing or tax-efficient drawdown — a good fee-only planner can be worth it. At your income you're in a high marginal tax bracket in BC (roughly 45–48% combined on the top slice), so tax planning alone can generate real savings that dwarf a planning fee. Since you're just retired, the decisions around when to take CPP/OAS, how to sequence withdrawals, and how to manage the RRIF conversion are exactly the kind that benefit from professional eyes.

Where it may not be worth it
If your situation is simple — mostly index funds in a TFSA and RRSP, a clear plan, and you're comfortable managing it — you can likely do it yourself with low-cost ETFs. Ongoing "assets under management" fees of 1–1.5% a year on a large portfolio are a meaningful drag; on $1M that's $10,000–15,000 annually, and it compounds against you. The fee structure matters more than the label.

The key distinction

Fee-only / advice-only planners charge an hourly or flat rate (often $1,500–3,500 for a full plan) and don't sell products or take commissions. For someone with a one-off need like a retirement drawdown plan, this is usually the best value.
Commission-based or AUM advisors get paid ongoing and may have incentives tied to what they sell you. Not inherently bad, but understand exactly how they're paid.

What I'd suggest
Consider a one-time fee-only retirement plan, then decide whether you want ongoing help. Ask any advisor: Are you a fiduciary? How exactly are you paid? Do you sell products? A good one will answer plainly.

One caveat: I don't know your full picture — assets, pension, spouse, health, goals — and this is general information, not personalised advice.
